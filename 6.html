ARCHITECTURE

A city, built slowly

In Kumasi, a new generation of architects is trading glass towers for thick walls, deep shade and the patience of earth.

WORDS ADA OKOROPHOTOGRAPHY KWESI MENSAH

On a Tuesday morning the scaffolding is bamboo and the blueprint is a sketch on the back of a paper bag. Ama Boateng, 38, kneels beside a half-finished courtyard and runs her palm along a wall of rammed earth that has taken eleven days to cure. “Concrete is fast,” she says. “But a house should be allowed to take its time.”

Boateng belongs to a growing circle of architects who are trading steel and glass for thick walls, deep shade and local materials. Their buildings are not nostalgic. They are quietly precise: timber joints cut by hand, roofs tuned to the prevailing wind, rooms that cool themselves without a single machine.

The movement has found unlikely allies. Developers, once wary of long build times, now report lower running costs and waiting lists of tenants. City councils are rewriting codes to reward walls that breathe.

Critics worry about scale. Can mud and bamboo house a million people? Boateng smiles at the question. “We are not against tall,” she says. “We are against hurry.”
